## 2021-09
@ sense-of-reform
Q A calligrapher disliked reading criticism of his own community. With what example was he answered?
* A teacher who points out faults in his lettering is helping him improve, not making him feel inferior :: Yes. Criticism builds a sense of reform.
- A customer who refuses to pay for poor work
- A rival who copies his designs
- A judge who awards a prize
R “If your teacher tells you about your shortcomings, will you say that my teacher is making me insecure and is creating a sense of inferiority in me?”
@ sense-of-reform
Q Ali pointed out a mistake in a ruling by Caliph Umar. How did Umar respond?
* He accepted it at once: “If it were not for Ali, Umar would have perished” :: Right. The adviser is better than the flatterer.
- He asked Ali to keep such views private
- He let the ruling stand to save face
- He referred the matter to others
R “Although Umar was the Caliph and ruler, he immediately accepted it and said: If it were not for Ali, Umar would have perished.”
@ guiding-one-to-a-purposeful-life
Q Everyone sets his watch by the city clock tower, yet few look to God’s guidance in the same way. What reason does the article give?
* People are serious about knowing the time, which serves their worldly aims, but not about the life to come :: Well read. Interest decides attention.
- The guidance is harder to see than a clock
- The clock tower is older
- People do not trust what is free
R “People are serious in wanting to know the time, but not the Word of God… they have no ambitions for the future life.”
@ teaching-the-teachers
Q After the war Japan learned industrial methods from others and later taught them to its own teachers. What lesson does the article draw?
* One must be willing to learn humbly from others before rising – “we have to lose in order to gain” :: Yes. Pride would have barred the way.
- Borrowed knowledge never lasts
- Teachers should guard their secrets
- Only large nations can progress
R “If it had chosen not to recognize the supremacy of others at that crucial point in its development, and had felt too proud to go to them for help, it could never have had such resounding successes.”
@ think-good-of-others
Q A minister advised the Caliph al-Mansur against a costly plan; the Caliph put the advice down to the minister’s background, went ahead, and had to stop. What does the article learn from this?
* Weigh advice as advice; do not assume it springs from ill-will :: Correct. The adviser may well be right.
- Never take advice from ministers
- Costly plans should always be completed
- A ruler’s first idea is best
R “We must look at someone’s advice as advice, we should not necessarily consider it as arising out of ill-will.”
@ fruits-of-sacrifice
Q What does the article say real sacrifice consists of?
* Doing small, unnoticed constructive work over a long time, without fame or reward – like a seed or a foundation brick :: Right. Not a single dramatic gesture.
- Giving away everything in one day
- Making one’s efforts widely known
- Working only where success is quick
R “Real sacrifice means taking a part so small and so inconspicuous in constructive work that it is almost invisible, and this, no matter how prolonged the period.”
@ cultivating-national-treasures
Q How does the article define “national character”?
* The capacity and will to put the nation’s interest above one’s own when the two clash :: Yes. Without it no nation advances.
- Pride in one’s language and dress
- Knowledge of national history
- Loyalty to a political party
R “It is, to put it simply, the capacity and the will to hold the interests of the nation supreme in every sphere.”
@ cultivating-national-treasures
Q The first editor of a great national work of biography wore himself out and did not see it finished. What does the article take from his example?
* Great undertakings need selfless, patient labour from people ready to remain obscure and not see the fruit :: Well reasoned. Others complete what one begins.
- Large projects should be avoided
- Editors should be better paid
- Such works are never worth the cost
R “One must be willing to remain obscure. One must keep on working tirelessly knowing quite well that he will not be able to see the fruit of his hard work.”
@ book-review
Q The book under review advises: “Live like the honeybee.” What does this mean?
* Take what is good from every situation and ignore the thorns, wasting no time on complaint :: Exactly. Extract the nectar and move on.
- Work without ever resting
- Live only among your own kind
- Store up more than you need
R “‘Live like the honeybee’. That is, extract what is good for you and leave what is unwanted. Do not waste your time in complaints and protests.”
@ the-word-of-god
Q Which qualities do the verses (3:133-134) ascribe to the God-fearing for whom Paradise is prepared?
* They give in good times and bad, restrain their anger, and forgive others :: Well read. “God loves those who do good works.”
- They are wealthy, learned and well known
- They avoid all contact with people
- They never make a mistake
R “For those who spend, both in prosperity and adversity, who restrain their anger and are forgiving towards their fellow men—God loves those who do good works.”
